# Phase 2 · Four groups, four questions about evaluation

Each group gets one question and four papers, with one marked as the **lead paper** that everyone in
the group reads properly. The other three are for the tools to work on.

You have three required tools and eight minutes to present. The tools are not decoration: each one
has to produce something you show us, and one of them has to be caught getting something wrong.

**Timing** — 45 minutes to prepare, then 8 minutes per group with 2 minutes of questions.

## Tool 1 · Research Rabbit or Connected Papers
kind: tool
tool: Pick one. Seed it with your lead paper.
must: Build the map around your lead paper and look at what sits next to it.
report: Show the map. Name one paper it surfaced that is **not** on your reading list, and say in one sentence whether it belongs there. Then say what the map cannot tell you — it measures similarity and co-citation, not quality or agreement.

## Tool 2 · Perplexity, or another chatbot you name
kind: tool
tool: Name the tool and the date you used it. "AI said" is not a source.
must: Ask it two specific questions about your lead paper — what the participants were, what was measured, what the effect was, what the authors say they cannot conclude.
report: Show one answer it got right and one it got wrong, could not support, or hedged into uselessness. Point at the page in the PDF that settles it. Every group is expected to find at least one of each.

## Tool 3 · NotebookLM
kind: tool
tool: Upload all four of your papers as sources.
must: Generate a synthesis of about 150 words with its inline citations switched on.
report: Show the synthesis, then trace one sentence of it back to the page it came from, on screen. Say whether the four papers actually agree — and if NotebookLM made them sound like they agree, say that.

## Group 1 · Do evaluation checklists predict learning?
kind: group
question: Evaluation frameworks tell us a material is good. Does anything show that materials scoring well on them teach better?
why: You will apply a checklist in this week's assignment, so you should be able to say where its authority comes from. This is also the most direct route to a midterm research question — the gap between "rated highly" and "learned more" is genuinely open.
lead: hubbard1988
refs: hubbard1988 | chapelle2001 | colpaert2006 | leakey2011
watch: Hubbard's framework and Chapelle's criteria are built on different foundations. Hubbard starts from Richards and Rodgers' approach–design–procedure; Chapelle derives hers from interactionist SLA. Ask what each one would miss.
- The claim your group will defend, in one falsifiable sentence.
- One criterion you would add to Chapelle's six for materials made after 2022, and why.
- One thing a checklist cannot see.

## Group 2 · What a Duolingo study can and cannot tell you
kind: group
question: App studies are the most-cited evidence about digital materials and the least controlled. What can this literature actually support?
why: Apps are what your classmates and students will reach for first, and what they will most often be asked to evaluate. The Duolingo case study is unusually honest about its own limits, which makes it a good model for your midterm methods section.
lead: loewen2019
refs: loewen2019 | heil2016 | rosellaguilar2017 | burston2014
watch: Note how many participants finished, how long the study ran, and what the outcome measure actually was. Then compare that with how the app's own marketing describes the same evidence.
- The claim your group will defend, in one falsifiable sentence.
- The comparison condition in your lead study, named exactly.
- One design change that would make the finding stronger, and what it would cost.

## Group 3 · When the material writes itself
kind: group
question: If a chatbot generates the text, the task and the feedback, what exactly are we evaluating — the tool, the prompt, or the teacher behind it?
why: This follows directly from last week's assignment, and it is where the field has the least settled evidence. It also sets up Week 14 on ethics and academic integrity. Notice that the pre-2022 dialogue-based literature studied rule-based systems, so part of it transfers and part does not.
lead: bibauw2019
refs: bibauw2019 | kohnke2023 | godwinjones2023 | warschauer2006
watch: Automated writing evaluation has been studied for twenty years and reached careful conclusions about feedback and revision. Ask how much of that applies to a general-purpose model that was never built for learners.
- The claim your group will defend, in one falsifiable sentence.
- One finding from the pre-2022 literature you think still holds, with your reason.
- One evaluation criterion that only makes sense for generated material.

## Group 4 · Authentic, multimodal, and still hard to judge
kind: group
question: "Authentic" and "multimodal" are the two most common selling points of digital material. What do they mean once you have to measure them?
why: This connects Week 5 on reading and Week 7 on multimodal input back to evaluation, and authenticity is the criterion students most often assert and least often define. The captioning meta-analysis gives you a rare case where the evidence is strong enough to argue with.
lead: gilmore2007
refs: gilmore2007 | monteroperez2013 | tomlinson2012 | winke2010
watch: Captions support comprehension more clearly than they support listening development. Keep those two outcomes apart when you report the meta-analysis.
- The claim your group will defend, in one falsifiable sentence.
- A workable definition of "authentic" that a colleague could apply to two materials and agree with you.
- One case where more modes made learning worse, with the condition attached.
