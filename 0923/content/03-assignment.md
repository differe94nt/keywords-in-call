# Phase 3 · Assignment — two materials, side by side

**Continue in your group using the matching path below: G1 → Path 1, G2 → Path 2, G3 → Path 3, G4 → Path 4.** Compare two materials or tasks for the same learners, proficiency level and learning goal. Build on the reading you presented in class.

**Your criteria are not handed to you — you take them from your reading.** Work out what that reading treats as mattering: what it inspects in a material, what questions it asks of one, what it warns against. Turn those into criteria you can actually apply to a screen in front of you. Give each one its source and page, and label any criterion you adapt or add yourselves.

**Present in three parts, in this order.**

1. **The two materials.** Introduce them briefly: who the learners are and what the goal is, the exact tasks you tried, versions or platforms, the date you tested, and what access costs.
2. **The criteria you adopted or formed.** State each criterion with its source and page, and one line on why the reading supports it. Path 1 works from Chapelle's six; Paths 2–4 form three criteria from the group's reading.
3. **The comparison.** Apply every criterion to both materials, with evidence taken from the materials themselves — a screenshot, a quotation, a task or an output. End with a recommendation for your specified learners, and one limitation or open question.

**Hand in one comparison table**, on a page or a few slides, with these columns: **criterion + source/page | Material A: evidence | Material B: evidence | judgement**.

**Four minutes.** A short trial supports judgements about design and usability. Claims about learning gains, retention or efficiency need learner evidence; if you have none, say what a later study would have to measure.

## Path 1 · Compare CALL tasks with Chapelle’s framework
kind: pathway
compare: Following Group 1, choose two digital tasks that address the same language-learning goal, such as two reading activities or two vocabulary lessons.
method: Apply all six criteria from Chapelle (2001), printed pp. 54–58: language learning potential, learner fit, meaning focus, authenticity, positive impact and practicality. Explain how task purpose affects your judgement; avoid treating an unweighted total score as proof of quality.
refs: chapelle2001

## Path 2 · Compare language-learning apps
kind: pathway
compare: Following Group 2’s reading review, choose two apps with lessons addressing the same skill, content and proficiency level.
method: Use three criteria grounded in the app reading your group presented. If you use Rosell-Aguilar, select and explain relevant framework criteria; if you use Heil or Loewen, explain how you derive design questions from the review or study.
refs: rosellaguilar2017 | heil2016 | loewen2019

## Path 3 · Compare generative AI writing tools
kind: pathway
compare: Following Group 3, choose two generative AI tools used for the same writing-support task, such as feedback on an argumentative paragraph.
method: Justify three criteria using your selected reading. Possible classroom adaptations include feedback accuracy, clarity of explanations and support for the learner’s own revision decisions. These examples are proposed evaluation questions, not a validated scale from the readings.
refs: kohnke2023 | godwinjones2023 | warschauer2006

## Path 4 · Compare multimodal teaching materials
kind: pathway
compare: Following Group 4, choose two multimodal lessons with the same learning goal, or two versions of a video activity that differ in caption or other support.
method: Use three criteria justified by your reading. Consider what each mode contributes to the goal, the support offered to the intended learners, and how the learning activity relates to the outcome being claimed. If using Gilmore, define the dimension of authenticity you evaluate.
refs: monteroperez2013 | winke2010 | gilmore2007

## Language learning potential
kind: criterion
from: chapelle2001
ask: Does the task offer useful opportunities to notice and work on language form while learning or using the language?
evidence: Show a task and its feedback, such as a writing activity that helps learners notice a recurring form. Explain the intended learning process. Inspecting an opportunity for learning does not demonstrate that learning occurred.

## Learner fit
kind: criterion
from: chapelle2001
ask: Can the intended learners engage with the language and complete the task with appropriate support?
evidence: Specify age, proficiency, language background, goals and setting. Compare the task’s language demands, instructions and available support with those characteristics.

## Meaning focus
kind: criterion
from: chapelle2001
ask: Does completing the activity require learners to understand or communicate a message?
evidence: Describe the message learners must interpret or convey, such as understanding directions to choose a route. Points and badges alone do not establish or rule out meaning focus. Distinguish your prediction about attention from observations of actual learners.

## Authenticity
kind: criterion
from: chapelle2001 | gilmore2007
ask: How does the activity relate to language uses that matter to these learners outside the exercise?
evidence: Define the dimension you are evaluating: text source, purpose, audience or task. For example, compare writing a message to arrange a meeting with completing isolated sentences. A real-world text does not automatically make every task built around it authentic.

## Positive impact
kind: criterion
from: chapelle2001
ask: What beneficial or adverse effects might the activity have on learners and teachers beyond the immediate language task?
evidence: Consider motivation, confidence, learning habits, autonomy and teacher workload. Separate effects you observed from those you predict, and identify what longer-term evidence would be needed.

## Practicality
kind: criterion
from: chapelle2001
ask: Are the resources needed to use the activity available in the intended teaching setting?
evidence: Record device and network requirements, account requirements, access costs, preparation time and class-size constraints. Give the date and access tier you checked.

## Data, privacy and access
kind: criterion
from: ortega2017
ask: What happens to learner data, and which learners can access and use the resource? This course extension combines access and equity questions with a separate review of data policies.
evidence: Ortega informs the access and equity questions. For data collection, retention, reuse and rights to submitted writing, consult the resource’s current policy and terms directly; cite the relevant sections and access date. Include accessibility and cost barriers. Mark information you cannot establish as unknown.

## What good comparison looks like
kind: note
- Apply your selected criteria to both materials: all six for Path 1, or three justified criteria for Paths 2–4. Explain the trade-offs behind your recommendation; a justified tie is acceptable.
- Where the two materials are equivalent, say so quickly and move on. The interesting part is where they diverge.
- Disagreement inside the group is worth reporting. If two of you rated authenticity differently, that tells us the criterion needs a sharper definition.
- Try both materials yourself. A short trial can support judgements about usability and task design; claims about retention or learning gains require learner data collected over an appropriate period.
