# Know the learner before choosing the tool

**Learner analysis** gathers evidence about what a learner can do, what they need to do, and the conditions that affect participation. **Differentiation** uses that evidence to adjust support, challenge, pace or task conditions while keeping the intended learning outcome clear. These are working definitions for this lesson, drawing on the [course handout](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1DQ5asdF6zp761tlNek-3ffc-UeGw7BIvnxqFwhC925w/edit) and [CAST’s UDL framework](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/).

A corpus gives you a **partial, task-specific performance profile**. It does not give you the whole person. Start with strengths; distinguish evidence, interpretation and what you still need to ask.

## What can the learner already do?
kind: lens
label: 01 · Readiness
text: Look for successful meaning-making as well as recurring difficulty: vocabulary, grammar, organization, interaction and task completion. Compare samples from the same learner and note differences in task and support.
action: Keep two short examples of a strength and two of a possible priority. A course level is contextual information, not a diagnosis of every skill.

## What are they working toward?
kind: lens
label: 02 · Goals and context
text: Identify the target task, audience and setting: describing a process, participating in a seminar, or writing a professional request. Use supplied metadata or reported goals; otherwise mark the goal as a teaching assumption.
action: Write one observable goal: “On a new picture, describe two objects and their spatial relationship clearly enough for a partner to identify them.”

## What helps or blocks participation?
kind: lens
label: 03 · Access and experience
text: Ask about time, devices, bandwidth, accessibility, prior schooling and language repertoire. An L1 label does not explain an individual error; a pause does not diagnose anxiety or a disability.
action: Separate corpus metadata from questions for a future learner interview. Offer a low-bandwidth or printed version when the digital feature is not the learning target.

## What choices and supports are useful?
kind: lens
label: 04 · Agency and support
text: Preferences and self-reported confidence can guide choices, but avoid assigning fixed visual, auditory or kinesthetic learner types. Pashler et al.’s review found insufficient evidence for matching instruction to such types.
action: Offer purposeful options, then observe their usefulness. For a speaking outcome, writing can support rehearsal, but the final evidence should still include speaking.
source: [Pashler et al. (2008), Learning styles: Concepts and evidence](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) · [CAST (2024)](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/)

## Comprehensible input
kind: theory
text: Krashen’s input hypothesis proposes acquisition through understanding language containing features beyond the learner’s current competence. It is a theoretical account, not a formula for selecting the next CEFR band.
action: Keep the message accessible with context or glosses; check comprehension before increasing demand.
source: [Krashen (1982), Principles and practice in second language acquisition, pp. 20–22](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf)

## Contingent support and fading
kind: theory
text: Scaffolding helps a learner manage a task they cannot yet complete independently. The ZPD and scaffolding are related concepts with different origins; Wood, Bruner and Ross introduced the scaffolding metaphor in their tutoring study.
action: Model once, offer a frame, then remove support when performance warrants it. Restore help if needed; fading is not an automatic countdown.
source: [Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976), pp. 89–100](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x) · [Course theory review](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1dwaIbj1AqpIuPJ0lkOfD3r-GowcDiXZzPYy5xRmP5qk/edit)

## Output, feedback and revision
kind: theory
text: Swain and Lapkin show how producing language can make learners notice a problem and reconsider their wording. Output can support learning; requiring a revision does not guarantee that learning occurred.
action: Give a partner information they need to act on. After a clarification question, revise the message and explain the change.
source: [Swain & Lapkin (1995), pp. 371–391](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/16.3.371) · [Course output handout](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1lA89_45rhxVnmhQF-asi1LfS6L_U2UebU0qxuQfGaog/edit)

## Design for variability
kind: theory
text: UDL offers multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression. It focuses on barriers and learner agency, rather than sorting students into fixed types.
action: Provide a choice of topic, readable materials and accessible response tools. Preserve the skill being assessed when offering alternatives.
source: [CAST (2024), UDL Guidelines 3.0](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/)

## Ivy · Rehearsal before public speaking
kind: case
text: In the course story, Ivy understands podcasts, wants to study abroad, reports speaking anxiety and finds long phone-screen reading uncomfortable. Those details are supplied by the story; they cannot be inferred from corpus grammar errors.
action: Offer a large-print image and private rehearsal, then a paired description with connector prompts. Fade prompts after a successful attempt; ask the listener to identify the image.
check: Can the listener recover the intended details from a new, less-supported description?

## Ken · Audience and paraphrase
kind: case
text: The course story describes strong topic knowledge and difficulty paraphrasing for a professional audience. A preference for fast interactive work is a reported preference, not a cognitive diagnosis.
action: Model how the same message changes for two audiences. Have Ken create two captions, explain the register choices and revise after peer feedback.
check: Do the versions preserve the message while using appropriate wording for each audience? Shared necessary terms are not automatically plagiarism.

## Mei · Smaller steps, accessible materials
kind: case
text: The course story reports limited vocabulary, slow decoding, test anxiety and unreliable Wi-Fi. Treat these as scenario facts for planning, not assumptions about learners with the same L1.
action: Use a printed image, a small word bank and a sequence from labels to sentences to a linked description. Keep practice low stakes; reduce support according to performance.
check: Can Mei describe a new image using the selected words and link ideas without the full frame?
