# Homework · Analyze a learner’s needs

**Choose one evidence route: a learner corpus, or an appropriate public speaking video.** Focus on one learner/speaker and build a provisional needs analysis from their actual language use. This is separate from the in-class lesson plan for Ivy, Ken or Mei; those scenarios do not count as homework data.

**Submit two pages or three slides, plus a small evidence appendix where permitted. Prepare a 4-minute explanation.** Use the same groups as in class. The main work is the analysis; finish with one justified teaching response. You do not need to contact the speaker, diagnose them or conduct a new learner experiment.

## 1 · Select and document the evidence
kind: deliverable
text: Corpus route: record corpus/version, anonymous learner or script ID, task and two short samples where available. Video route: select one speaker and two clearly timestamped segments of around 30–60 seconds each, with enough connected speech to analyze. Two parts of one recording are acceptable; state that limitation.
- Give the source URL, title, access date and task context. Use a neutral label such as Speaker A in your analysis; keep the source link so the evidence is retrievable.
- Include short permitted excerpts, or your own analytic notes with file/line IDs or start–end timestamps. For video, prepare a brief transcript of the relevant turns and check it against the audio.
- State what information is missing and whether speech may be rehearsed, edited or constrained by an exam task. Do not mix the interviewer’s or partner’s words with the selected learner’s data.

## 2 · Build the needs analysis
kind: deliverable
text: Identify one strength and two possible priorities. For each, connect an observation to its evidence and explain why it matters for a stated task or a clearly labelled assumed learning goal.
- Support one priority with a transparent count and denominator, or a documented comparison across the two samples. Include successful uses, not just errors.
- Separate observed performance, interpretation and unknown background. A short clip cannot establish general proficiency, anxiety, motivation, disability or a fixed learning style.
- Use published examiner comments only after writing your own observations. Identify whether the comments support or complicate your interpretation. Report any official score as source information, not a score your group has independently validated.

## 3 · Propose a teaching response
kind: deliverable
text: Choose one priority and propose one short learning activity, with a scaffold and a new less-supported check. Explain the link between the evidence, one relevant theory/framework and your design. A full lesson plan or working AI application is not required for this homework.
- Include a learner-facing instruction and a small usable example. Explain what the learner would practise and what you would look for on the new task.
- If AI helped, record the tool/date, prompt and one suggestion you checked or revised. You can complete the assignment without AI.
- End with one limitation and one question for further evidence. This could become a final-project needs analysis, materials redesign or evaluation study.

## Can I choose another YouTube clip?
kind: video_guide
text: Yes, if it provides enough contextualized language from one identifiable speaker for a cautious performance analysis. Prefer an educational interview or a speaker’s own language-learning practice with a stated L2 context. Official speaking examples above are easier to justify than a random entertainment clip.
- Check that you can hear the selected speaker clearly and distinguish their turns. Avoid dubbed, heavily edited or fully scripted performances when your claim concerns spontaneous language use.
- Do not infer someone’s L1, nationality or learner status from appearance or accent. If the source does not establish a language-learning context, choose another source or discuss a narrowly defined performance-analysis goal with the teacher.
- Record the URL, channel/title, task and exact start–end times. Link to the original; do not re-upload the clip or copy a full transcript into the submission.
- Automatic captions can omit, repair or invent words. Check each relevant line by listening; mark uncertain words as unclear. If no usable audio is available, choose another clip or use the corpus route.

## How to analyze a clip
kind: video_guide
text: Use the same evidence → interpretation → need sequence as for a corpus, adding timestamps and audio checks.
- **Listen for meaning first:** identify what the speaker is trying to do and one thing they accomplish successfully.
- **Select and transcribe:** choose two 30–60-second segments; transcribe only the selected learner’s relevant turns. Keep useful repetition, self-correction and pauses; use [unclear] rather than guessing.
- **Choose two features:** for example, reference clarity, organization of an answer, word choice, a recurring grammatical form, or how the speaker responds to a clarification request. Avoid trying to diagnose everything.
- **Check the pattern:** compare successful and difficult instances. For grammar, count relevant opportunities; for interaction, cite the sequence and response. Do not equate every pause or self-correction with a deficit. If you count pauses, define the threshold and measure from the audio.
- **State a bounded need:** “In these two answers, Speaker A could benefit from …” Explain what more you would need to know before planning ongoing instruction. Judge pronunciation by communicative clarity in context, not resemblance to a preferred accent.

## What a strong submission demonstrates
kind: rubric
text: Four equally weighted criteria, scored 0–2 each (8 points). 0 = missing or unsupported; 1 = partly justified; 2 = explicit and traceable.
- **Evidence:** the same learner/speaker is traceable through source references, task context and file/line IDs or timestamps; transcription and counts are checked.
- **Analysis:** strengths and priorities follow from examples; successful instances, unknowns and assumptions are visible.
- **Teaching response:** one priority, learning goal, theory and feasible activity fit together.
- **Limits and evaluation:** the new task measures the stated goal; the proposal does not claim the learner has improved or generalize beyond the samples.
