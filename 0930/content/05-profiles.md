# Read the learner. Design the lesson.

**In-class group task:** choose Ivy, Ken or Mei. Read the whole story before turning the card. Use the questions on the reverse to agree on one priority, then design a 10–15-minute lesson using the theories we have learned. More than one group may choose the same learner and compare their decisions.

These are the instructor’s **teaching scenarios from the profile PDF**. Treat the supplied background and self-reports as scenario information. Do not add a diagnosis or invent an assessment result. The reverse sides provide questions to discuss; your group supplies the lesson plan.

## Ivy
kind: profile
subtitle: The reluctant speaker with big plans
story_a: Ivy is a 19-year-old English major in her second year. She grew up in Taichung speaking Mandarin at home and learned English from Grade 3. She breezes through podcasts and YouTube interviews and can summarize the main ideas for classmates, but when it’s her turn to speak in seminars, her voice drops. She often pauses to translate in her head first.
story_b: Ivy wants to apply for a semester abroad next year. She follows student vlogs from the UK and has a Pinterest board of “must-visit bookshops.” To save for the trip, she works 12 hours/week at a café. She loves visual study aids (color-coded slides, infographics), and keeps a vocabulary notebook where she underlines connectors like however and on the other hand. Tests make her hands cold; she usually finishes writing tasks but leaves speaking tasks for last.
story_c: Her eyesight is fine but she sometimes gets headaches reading long PDFs on a phone screen; she prefers printed handouts or slides with larger fonts.
- Which existing strengths could help Ivy reach her study-abroad goal? Point to details in the story.
- Which single language-performance goal would you choose for this lesson, and why?
- Which theory explains the support you propose? What would Ivy actually do at each step?
- How will your plan account for her time and access preferences while preserving the target skill?
- What new, less-supported task would show whether your support helped?

## Ken
kind: profile
subtitle: The tech helper who writes like a researcher
story_a: Ken is 21 and double-majoring in English and Computer Science. At home his parents speak Cantonese; at university he uses Mandarin and English daily. He reads fast—especially tech blogs and API docs—and he can explain complex ideas clearly to peers. In group projects he likes to be the “tool person,” setting up shared folders and formatting documents.
story_b: Ken’s goal is an internship at a multinational company. He wants to sound more professional and less “Reddit casual.” He enjoys interactive tasks (pair coding, live polls) and short videos over long readings. When he writes, he cites sources correctly but struggles to paraphrase; his summaries sometimes feel like stitched quotes.
story_c: He has no medical issues but admits he gets impatient when activities move slowly. He’s happy to mentor classmates on tech, as long as the expectations are clear.
- Which strength can your plan build on, and which need matters most for Ken’s internship goal?
- What language product would make the learning goal observable?
- Which theory explains how your activity should help, beyond simply choosing a technology topic?
- How will you provide enough challenge while ensuring Ken works on his own learning need?
- What evidence would distinguish an attractive finished product from independent control of the target skill?

## Mei
kind: profile
subtitle: The returning learner rebuilding confidence
story_a: Mei is 23 and recently returned to university after one year of full-time work in a bakery. She speaks Vietnamese at home and uses Mandarin in class. She’s careful and diligent: she arrives early, takes neat notes, and asks for extra practice.
story_b: Her reading is A2–B1; she looks up many words and sometimes loses the thread of a paragraph. She smiles during pair work but prefers short turns and clear sentence frames to start. Her dream is to pass the B1 certificate by the end of the year so she can apply for a tourism program.
story_c: Mei reports test anxiety (“My mind goes blank during quizzes”). She also says she learns best when teachers chunk tasks and provide examples first. She has an older laptop and spotty Wi-Fi at home, so large video files are hard to stream.
- What does the story establish about Mei’s strengths, goal and participation conditions?
- Which one need is manageable in a short lesson? What would success look like?
- Which theory supports your sequence of learning activities, and how does it explain the support?
- How will the same learning goal remain achievable with her available technology?
- When would you reduce or restore support, and how would you check performance on new content?

## Make one learner-based lesson plan
kind: workshop
text: Spend about 20 minutes: read and identify evidence (5), choose a goal and theory (5), then draft the activity sequence and check (10). Use the Digital design phase for tool ideas after you have chosen the learning goal.
- **Learner and evidence:** name the profile and cite two details that justify your chosen priority.
- **Goal and theory:** state one observable goal and explain one or two relevant concepts. Name the learning mechanism, not just the theorist.
- **Lesson sequence:** outline a model/input stage, learner practice, feedback or revision, and a new less-supported task. Include actual learner instructions and one example.
- **Differentiation and check:** identify the support, when it changes, an access alternative and two or three success indicators.

## Share and challenge the design
kind: workshop
text: Hand in one slide or one page for the in-class task. Explain your plan to another group in two minutes; give them one minute to identify an unsupported assumption or an activity that does not serve the goal. Revise one part before submitting.
- Use the chain: **profile detail → learning need → theory → activity decision → evidence of success**.
- If you use AI, first write your own priority and goal; check that its proposed activities follow the learner story. Keep the revision you make.
- Keep this scenario-based plan separate from homework: the homework learner comes from a corpus or an appropriate public speaking example, not these cards.
