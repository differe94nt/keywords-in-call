# Learner language: people vs AI

Today you analyse two real learners — one writing, one speaking — the way a teacher would: find the errors, decide which matter most, and propose how to teach them. Then you give the same task to AI tools and compare. The question is not "who finds more errors?" but **"whose analysis would help this learner more — and why?"**

## Error analysis in a nutshell
kind: section
Corder's argument still frames this work: learners' errors are evidence of the system they are building, so they tell the teacher where the learner is, not only what went wrong.
refs: corder1967

## Error or mistake?
kind: card
An **error** comes from what the learner does not yet know — it tends to recur. A **mistake** is a slip the learner could correct if it were pointed out — it is often inconsistent. Look for evidence: does the same form appear correctly elsewhere in the sample? Did the learner correct herself?
refs: corder1967

## The five steps
kind: card
- **Identify:** mark each place where the learner's language differs from what the context needs.
- **Describe:** say what kind of difference it is — grammar, word choice, spelling, punctuation, organisation, task requirement.
- **Explain:** suggest *why* it might happen — but say "might". You cannot prove a cause from one text.
- **Evaluate:** which errors block meaning (global) and which do not (local)? Which recur?
- **Prioritise and teach:** pick what to work on first, and why.

## Look beyond the sentence
kind: card
Teachers often catch what tools miss: Did the learner do **what the task asked**? Is the text **organised** for its genre? In speaking, did she **get her message across**, ask for help, or repair her own speech? Fillers and self-corrections are part of normal speech — not errors to correct.

## Start with strengths
kind: card
Before listing problems, write down two things each learner does well. A teaching plan built only on errors usually aims at the wrong target — and real teachers have to give feedback a learner can bear to read.
