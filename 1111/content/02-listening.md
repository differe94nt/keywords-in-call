# Listening

Listening happens in real time: learners cannot re-read, and a word they know in writing can disappear in fast connected speech. Research points to two levers you can design for — **how learners manage their listening** and **what support the input gives them** — and recent work asks what AI can add to both.

## What research says
kind: section

## Teaching learners how to listen
kind: paper
badge: Classic · 2010
did: Over a semester, 106 learners in six French-as-a-second-language classes listened to the same texts the same number of times. The experimental group (59) was guided through the processes of successful listening — predicting and planning, monitoring, evaluating, problem solving. The control group (47) was not.
found: The guided group outperformed the control group on the final comprehension measure. Within the guided group, **less skilled listeners gained more than more skilled ones**. Learners' awareness of their own listening processes grew over the semester.
means: Playing the audio more often is not the same as teaching listening. Build in prediction, partner comparison and reflection between listens.
refs: vandergrift2010

## Captioned video
kind: paper
badge: Classic · 2013
did: A meta-analysis of 18 studies on L2 video with L2 captions: 15 studies measured listening comprehension, 10 measured vocabulary learning.
found: Captions had a **large effect** on both listening comprehension and vocabulary learning. The type of test used changed how large the listening effect looked; learners' proficiency level did not.
means: Captions are worth using — but decide what you are testing. A comprehension test on the content is not the same as a test of listening without support.
refs: montero2013

## A chatbot for decoding practice
kind: paper
badge: Recent · 2024
did: A flipped course for *decoding* — recognising words in connected speech — worked better than a conventional course but overloaded the teacher. The team built a chatbot as a 24/7 practice partner and compared a fully online chatbot course (46 students) with the flipped course (43).
found: The study found **no significant difference between the groups on the decoding post-test**. The authors report that the chatbot course better supported **behavioural and emotional engagement**; read the paper for when in the course each difference appeared.
means: "No significant difference" does not prove the two courses are equal. The design suggests a chatbot can take on repetitive practice and reminders that would otherwise depend on the teacher — the study did not measure teacher workload directly.
refs: huang2024

## Does AI make listening and speaking better?
kind: paper
badge: Recent · 2025
did: A review of how AI — spoken dialogue systems, intelligent personal assistants and generative AI — can support teaching, learning and assessing L2 listening and speaking.
found: AI can refine existing practice and offer new solutions, but also brings drawbacks; the authors set out practical considerations for teachers and directions for research.
means: Start from an existing good practice and ask what AI improves. Name one drawback you will manage. *(Also useful for speaking groups.)*
refs: goh2025

## Activity ideas
kind: section
These are **classroom adaptations inspired by the studies** — the exact activities were not tested in them. For seven more listening activity types, and the websites that can run them, see the **Activity board**.

## The listening cycle
kind: activity
do:
- **Predict:** learners see the title or a picture and predict words and ideas.
- **Listen and check:** first listen; note what was confirmed.
- **Compare:** partners compare notes and plan what to listen for.
- **Listen again:** check the plan; solve the problem spots.
- **Reflect:** what helped you understand this time?
role: AI drafts prediction prompts and a short script at the right level from your topic; a free text-to-speech voice or your own recording turns it into audio.
check: Does the script sound like speech, not like writing read aloud?
refs: vandergrift2010

## Captions with a plan
kind: activity
do:
- First viewing with full captions, focusing on meaning.
- Second viewing with captions off; learners note what they now catch.
- A short task that uses the content.
role: AI drafts the comprehension questions and a captions-on / captions-off worksheet.
check: This sequence is a design choice — the meta-analysis shows captions help, not that this order is best. Watch what your learners can do without captions.
refs: montero2013

## Hear it, type it
kind: activity
do:
- Learners hear short chunks of fast speech (*want to → wanna*, linked words) and type what they hear.
- Hints appear on request: number of words, first letters, the written form.
- A final round with new chunks and no hints.
role: AI builds the interactive page; the browser's built-in text-to-speech can read each chunk aloud.
check: Synthetic voices vary between devices, and one voice means one accent. Add recordings of real speakers where you can.
refs: huang2024

## Mistakes to avoid
kind: card
- Playing the audio again and again with no new purpose.
- Comprehension questions that test reading or memory more than listening.
- AI scripts that sound written, not spoken — no hesitations, no natural speed.
- Only one voice and one accent; learners will meet many.

## Revisit your draft
kind: questions
- Where in your activity do learners *manage* their listening, not only listen?
- What support (captions, script, hints) do they get — and when is it removed?
- Which study supports your main design decision?
