# Reading

Digital reading gives teachers tools print never had: words that explain themselves when clicked, questions generated in seconds, a text rewritten at an easier level on demand. Research shows which of these help — and recent work warns that "easier" text is not always easier to understand.

## What research says
kind: section

## Pictures, text or video glosses?
kind: paper
badge: Classic · 1996
did: Three studies with 160 university learners of German reading in a hypermedia program whose words could be annotated with text, pictures or video.
found: Learners picked up more vocabulary incidentally than expected (25% on production tests, 77% on recognition). Words annotated with **pictures plus text** were remembered significantly better than words with video plus text or text alone, and the type of annotation learners looked up tended to be the cue they used to remember the word.
means: For concrete target words, a picture plus a short definition is a strong gloss. Video is not automatically better.
refs: chunplass1996

## What glosses do for reading
kind: paper
badge: Classic · 2008
did: A meta-analysis of 11 studies on computer-mediated glosses in L2 reading.
found: Glosses had a **medium effect on reading comprehension** and a **large effect on incidental vocabulary learning**; effects varied with learners' level, the type of text and how learning was assessed.
means: Glossing helps vocabulary most. Choose which words to gloss on purpose, and match the gloss to your learners' level.
refs: abraham2008

## Can ChatGPT write reading questions?
kind: paper
badge: Recent · 2024
did: The researchers asked ChatGPT to write multiple-choice reading comprehension items targeting given reading subskills and materials, then compared them with human-written items, and gathered views through questionnaires, expert judgement and interviews.
found: ChatGPT's items were **comparable to human-written items on the psychometric measures**, and reviewers saw its potential as a test developer and a teaching assistant for reading.
means: AI can draft questions aimed at specific subskills (main idea, detail, inference, vocabulary in context). You still check every key and every distractor.
refs: lin2024

## Is AI-simplified text easier to understand?
kind: paper
badge: Recent · 2025
did: The study compared L2 readers' comprehension of three versions of a passage: the original, a version simplified by human experts, and a version simplified automatically with **Cleartext**, a machine-learning simplification tool — not ChatGPT.
found: Once readers' **topic familiarity** was taken into account, **neither simplified version was easier to understand** than the original in this study.
means: Shorter words and sentences do not guarantee comprehension; what readers already know about the topic matters. The study does not show that today's chatbots cannot help — it shows you should test any AI-simplified text with learners (for example, by asking them to think aloud) before relying on it.
refs: murphyodo2025

## Activity ideas
kind: section
These are **classroom adaptations inspired by the studies** — the exact activities were not tested in them. For seven more reading activity types, and the websites that can run them, see the **Activity board**.

## Picture-glossed reading
kind: activity
do:
- Choose 6–8 target words in a short text.
- Learners read; clicking a word shows a picture and a short definition.
- After reading, a quick recall task with the pictures, then one with words only.
role: AI drafts the definitions at the learners' level and describes a picture for each word; it can build the clickable page.
check: Do the pictures show the meaning clearly? Are the definitions simpler than the words they explain?
refs: chunplass1996 | abraham2008

## Questions by subskill
kind: activity
do:
- AI drafts one question each for main idea, detail, inference and vocabulary in context.
- The teacher checks and fixes them.
- After answering, learners write one question of their own for another group.
role: AI drafts the items and the answer key.
check: Can a learner answer any question *without* reading the text? Is there exactly one correct answer?
refs: lin2024

## Original or simplified? Explore it
kind: activity
do:
- Before reading, learners meet the topic through pictures, a short video or a quick discussion.
- Half the class reads the original text, half an AI-simplified version.
- Groups compare: what did each half understand, and where did they get stuck?
- The class writes one question to test next time — for example, *Would the simplified text still help without the topic preparation?*
role: AI produces the simplified version and the pre-reading prompts.
check: This is a classroom exploration, not an experiment — both halves had the same preparation, so you cannot tell whether the topic or the language helped. Did the simplification remove meaning the task needs?
refs: murphyodo2025

## Mistakes to avoid
kind: card
- Glossing so many words that the text becomes a dictionary.
- Questions that can be answered from general knowledge.
- Assuming a simplified text is an easier text.
- Uploading copyrighted textbook texts to public AI tools.

## Revisit your draft
kind: questions
- What will learners understand, notice or learn from the text — and how will you know?
- What support do they get while reading, and does it target the right words?
- If AI changes the text, how will you check it is still worth reading?
