# Tasks, problems and projects

This week starts with **tasks**. The way this course is built — choose a skill, try the tools, build an activity, present it, get feedback — is a **task cycle**, and today's chatbot build is one too. This page gives the research behind **task-based language teaching (TBLT)** and compares it with two close relatives: **problem-based learning (PBL)** and **project-based learning (PjBL)**. Then we read Vahid Aryadoust's work on assessment and AI (pages 2–8) and build a chatbot (page 9).

Both relatives are often shortened to "PBL". On this site, **PBL** means problem-based and **PjBL** means project-based.

## What counts as a task?
kind: section
"Task" means different things to different writers — some textbooks use it for any exercise. Start with the definitions.

## Four criteria
kind: card
Ellis (2009) calls an activity a task when:
- the **primary focus is on meaning**, not on practising a form;
- there is some kind of **gap** — information, opinion or reasoning — to close;
- learners rely mainly on **their own language resources** to do it;
- there is a **clearly defined outcome** other than the use of language.
refs: ellis2009

## Task-based or task-supported?
kind: card
- **Task-based (Long):** the whole course is built on tasks — a needs analysis finds the real-world *target tasks*, *pedagogic tasks* move from simple to complex, each series ends in an *exit task*, and assessment is task-based too. There is no grammar syllabus.
- **Task-supported:** tasks are used to practise a grammar or vocabulary syllabus.
- **Ellis's middle way:** a syllabus that is mainly task-based, with task-supported lessons added for language problems that tasks alone do not fix.
refs: long2016 | ellis2017

## Kinds of task
kind: card
- **Unfocused** tasks elicit language in general; **focused** tasks are designed so that learners have to process a particular form.
- **Input-based** tasks (listen or read, then act) do not require speaking or writing; **output-based** tasks do.
- Input-based tasks suit **beginners**, large classes and learners used to traditional teaching; output-based tasks are needed for higher proficiency.
refs: ellis2017

## Real-world or pedagogic?
kind: card
A **real-world task** copies something learners will do outside class, such as booking a hotel room. A **pedagogic task**, such as *spot the difference*, never happens in real life but produces real interaction — turn-taking, repairing misunderstandings. Ellis argues that a needs analysis of target tasks is hard to carry out in many state school systems in East Asia, where learners' future uses of English are hard to predict.
refs: ellis2017

## A lesson as a task cycle
kind: section
Willis (1996) organises a task-based lesson in three phases. Form is dealt with **before and after** the task, not during it.
refs: willis1996

## Pre-task
kind: step
do: Introduce the topic and the task; activate useful words and phrases; learners may hear or see others doing a similar task.

## Task cycle
kind: step
do:
- **Task:** pairs or groups do the task, using whatever language they have.
- **Planning:** they prepare to report to the class — this is where attention to accuracy naturally rises.
- **Report:** they present what they did or decided.

## Language focus
kind: step
do: Learners analyse language from the task texts or recordings, then practise it.

## Suggested answers for seven activities
kind: reveal
- **L10 Listening jigsaw — task.** Each group holds different information (gap); the class must rebuild the whole talk (outcome).
- **S7 Story co-build — task**, output-based. The rule about tense and viewpoint adds a light focus on form.
- **S5 Mini-debate — almost a task.** There is an opinion gap, but no outcome. Add one: the pair must agree on a joint recommendation.
- **L3 Minimal-pair detective — exercise.** The focus is on a form (sounds) and there is no outcome beyond the answers. It is valuable task-supported practice.
- **R5 Headline match — exercise.** It becomes a task if learners choose which three stories go on a class news page and justify the choice.
- **W9 Email genre swap — focused exercise.** It becomes a task if learners write a real request to a real reader and the reply decides the outcome.
- **R10 Fact-check chain — close to a task.** Give it an outcome: "Decide whether to share this post, and tell a friend why."

## What the research says about TBLT
kind: section
Defining papers, a critique and the replies, a meta-analysis and reviews. Free full texts are linked where they are legal; the others are through the NTU library.

## Task-based language teaching: Sorting out the misunderstandings
kind: card
badge: Classic · 2009
key:
- Defines a task and argues there is **no single** task-based approach.
- Answers critics (Widdowson, Seedhouse, Sheen, Swan): many criticisms come from misunderstanding what a task is.
- Task-based teaching can be used **alongside** form-focused teaching, not only instead of it.
- Ends with genuine implementation problems seen in evaluation studies.
design:
- Use the four criteria to check your own activities.
basis: Abstract. The four criteria are the paper's definition of a task — check the exact wording there.
refs: ellis2009

## Legislation by hypothesis: The case of task-based instruction
kind: card
badge: Classic · critique · 2005
key:
- The claim that grammar is acquired by noticing during communication rests on **unproved hypotheses**.
- Tasks may develop command of language learners **already know**, but are weaker for teaching **new** language — especially with little class time and no English outside class.
- Warns against swinging between meaning-only and form-only teaching.
design:
- Read it with Taiwan's class hours in mind: where does new language come from in your task?
basis: Abstract.
refs: swan2005

## In defense of tasks and TBLT: Nonissues and real issues
kind: card
badge: Classic · 2016
key:
- Defines genuine TBLT as task-based throughout: needs analysis → target tasks → pedagogic tasks sequenced by complexity → task-based, criterion-referenced tests.
- Answers **14 criticisms**; most, he argues, are non-issues.
- Lists ten methodological principles — for example, learning by doing, rich input, focus on form, negative feedback.
- **Real issues** that remain: how to measure task complexity, how to assess task performance and whether it transfers to other tasks, and teacher education.
design:
- If a learner can do one task, can they do a similar one? This is the transfer question, and it applies to any task-based test or chatbot.
basis: Full text.
link: [⬇ Free PDF (Cambridge, free to read)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/ED5590DB727AE82E0A98AC05264B83E1/S0267190515000057a.pdf/div-class-title-in-defense-of-tasks-and-tblt-nonissues-and-real-issues-div.pdf)
refs: long2016

## Position paper: Moving task-based language teaching forward
kind: card
badge: Classic · 2017
key:
- Ten real issues, from "what is a task?" to feedback timing and teacher education.
- Distinguishes focused/unfocused and input-/output-based tasks; calls for more **input-based** tasks.
- Needs-based syllabuses are hard to apply in East Asian state schools.
- Proposes a **hybrid** syllabus: mainly task-based, with task-supported teaching for stubborn language problems.
design:
- Should your bot give feedback during the task or after it? Ellis treats this as an open question — decide and justify.
basis: Full text (author's submitted version).
link: [⬇ Author's version (Curtin University repository)](https://curate.curtin.edu.au/articles/Position_paper_Moving_task-based_language_teaching_forward/31664641)
refs: ellis2017

## Options in a task-based language-teaching curriculum
kind: card
badge: Recent · 2021
key:
- Compares four TBLT curricula: **Prabhu, Willis, Long and Ellis**.
- Offers questions to evaluate a curriculum and lists the options at each design stage.
- Notes the tension between what SLA theory says is needed and what a teaching context allows; asks for **flexibility**.
basis: Abstract.
refs: ellis2021

## TBLT implementation and evaluation: A meta-analysis
kind: card
badge: Meta-analysis · 2019
key:
- **52 studies** of TBLT programmes running over time in real classrooms.
- Overall effect on learning outcomes: **d = 0.93** — positive and strong.
- Effects varied with region, type of institution, whether a needs analysis was done, and how many cycles of implementation.
- Teachers' and learners' perceptions were positive.
design:
- Evidence for whole programmes, not single tasks — a reply to Swan's 2005 point that long-term evidence was lacking.
basis: Abstract.
refs: bryfonski2019

## The implementation of CLT and TBLT in the Asia-Pacific region
kind: card
badge: Review · 2011
key:
- Constraints at three levels: **conceptual** (local values, misconceptions), **classroom** (students, teachers, management, resources) and **societal–institutional** (curricula, exams).
- Successful TBLT in Asia needs **adaptation** to local practice.
design:
- Name the exam and class-size constraints your activity must work within.
basis: Abstract.
refs: butler2011

## Technology-mediated TBLT
kind: card
badge: Reviews · 2016 & 2020
key:
- **Ziegler (2016):** state-of-the-art review of how technology-mediated tasks support L2 development and performance, and how technology helps research task design and implementation.
- **Chong & Reinders (2020):** a grounded-theory synthesis of **16 classroom studies (2002–2017)** — the characteristics, affordances and limitations of technology-mediated TBLT, and what makes it work.
design:
- A chatbot or AI tool does not make an activity a task; the gap and the outcome do.
basis: Abstracts.
link: [⬇ Chong & Reinders, free (Language Learning & Technology, CC BY-NC-ND)](https://hdl.handle.net/10125/44739)
refs: ziegler2016 | chong2020

## Problem-based learning (PBL)
kind: section
PBL began in medical education and spread to many disciplines. Learners work in groups on a real problem, find out what they need to know, and propose a solution.
refs: barrows1996 | savery2006 | strobel2009

## Overview of problem-based learning: Definitions and distinctions
kind: card
badge: Classic · 2006
key:
- PBL is a **learner-centred** approach in which learners do research, connect theory and practice, and apply knowledge and skills to develop a **viable solution to a defined problem**.
- Compares PBL with other experiential approaches, such as project-based and case-based learning, and names its challenges.
design:
- Read his distinctions before you call your unit "PBL".
basis: Abstract.
link: [⬇ Free (Interdisciplinary Journal of Problem-Based Learning)](https://doi.org/10.7771/1541-5015.1002)
refs: savery2006

## The guidance debate
kind: card
badge: Classic debate · 2006–2007
key:
- **Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006):** minimally guided instruction — they include PBL — is less effective and efficient than guided instruction; the advantage of guidance shrinks only when learners already know a lot.
- **Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007):** PBL is not unguided discovery — it is **heavily scaffolded**, and it also aims at goals such as collaboration and self-directed learning.
design:
- Both sides agree on one thing: novices need guidance. Plan the scaffolds — models, sentence frames, checkpoints.
basis: Abstracts.
refs: kirschner2006 | hmelo2007

## When is PBL more effective?
kind: card
badge: Meta-synthesis · 2009
key:
- A qualitative synthesis of earlier meta-analyses comparing PBL with conventional teaching.
- PBL did better on **long-term retention**, **skill development** and **student and teacher satisfaction**.
- Conventional teaching did better on **short-term retention** measured by standardised board exams.
design:
- What you measure decides which approach "wins" — the same point Aryadoust makes about validity.
basis: Abstract.
link: [⬇ Free (Interdisciplinary Journal of Problem-Based Learning)](https://doi.org/10.7771/1541-5015.1046)
refs: strobel2009

## PBL in a web-based English reading course
kind: card
badge: Study · 2018
key:
- Two university classes were randomly assigned to a **web-based PBL** reading course or to teacher lectures.
- The PBL group, which searched the internet for solutions to a problem, scored significantly higher on a reading comprehension post-test.
- Questionnaires showed more active learning.
design:
- The author works at National Taiwan Ocean University. Only two classes were compared, so the teacher and class effects cannot be separated — a design to replicate, not a final answer.
basis: Abstract.
refs: lin2018

## Project-based language learning
kind: card
badge: Classic · 2005
key:
- Projects can develop **language, content and skills** together, but ESL students do not always see their value.
- The **Project Framework** makes the three goals explicit so that students see what they are learning.
design:
- Tell learners what language they are learning through the project, not only what they are making.
basis: Abstract.
refs: beckett2005

## Project-based learning (PjBL)
kind: section
Learners work over a longer period towards a **product** for a real audience — a guide, a video, a campaign, a website — and learn language, content and skills on the way.
refs: stoller2019 | kokotsaki2016

## Project-based learning in second language acquisition
kind: card
badge: Classic · 2019
key:
- PjBL has been recommended for **purposeful language learning for more than three decades**, and for combining language and content aims, literacy and research skills, and service learning.
- Reported benefits: better language skills (including reading informational texts), deeper content learning, real-life skills, sustained motivation and engagement, and positive self-concept.
- Most of this support comes from **teachers' reports**; controlled studies are few.
design:
- Plan the language learners will need at each stage of the project — do not leave it to chance.
basis: Abstract (chapter in an edited book).
refs: stoller2019

## Project-based learning: A review of the literature
kind: card
badge: Review · 2016
key:
- PjBL is characterised by learners' **autonomy, investigations, goal-setting, collaboration, communication and reflection** within real-world practice.
- Most studies were quasi-experimental without random allocation, so a **causal link** to better outcomes cannot be established with certainty.
- What helps: digital technology, good group processes, teacher **scaffolding**, a balance between direct instruction and inquiry, and **well-aligned assessment**.
design:
- Decide in advance how the product, the process and the language will each be assessed.
basis: Abstract.
refs: kokotsaki2016

## Effects of project-based learning: a meta-analysis
kind: card
badge: Meta-analysis · 2019
key:
- **30 journal articles (1998–2017)**, 46 comparisons, 12,585 students in 189 schools in nine countries.
- PjBL versus traditional teaching: **d+ = 0.71**, a medium-to-large effect on academic achievement.
- The effect varied with subject area, school location, hours of instruction and **information technology support** — not with educational stage or group size.
design:
- This covers school subjects in general, not language learning specifically. Use it as background, not as proof for an English class.
basis: Abstract.
refs: chen2019

## Three approaches compared
kind: section
This table is our comparison, drawn from the sources on this page. All three are learner-centred and start from meaning; they differ in **what the work is organised around** and **what the outcome is for**.

| | TBLT | Problem-based (PBL) | Project-based (PjBL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Began in | Second language acquisition research and language teaching | Medical education | General education; in language teaching for more than 30 years |
| Organised around | A **task**: a meaning-focused activity with a gap and an outcome | A **problem**: complex and open, with no single answer | A **product** for an audience: a guide, video, website or event |
| Ends with | The task outcome: a decision, a map, a plan | A proposed solution | A finished product, shown or presented |
| Main goal | Language development through using the language | Content knowledge, problem-solving, collaboration, self-directed learning | Language, content and skills together; motivation |
| Role of language | The target of learning | The medium; in a language class, medium and target | Medium and target — best made explicit to learners |
| Typical length | One lesson, or a sequence ending in an exit task | Several sessions to several weeks | Several weeks to a term |
| Teacher | Designs and sequences tasks; plans the focus on form | Facilitates and scaffolds the inquiry | Guides, scaffolds, balances direct teaching and inquiry |
| Assessed by | Task completion, sometimes plus language quality | The solution and the process | The product, the process and the language |
| Evidence | TBLT programmes: d = 0.93 | Better long-term retention and skills; weaker on short-term exams | School subjects: d+ = 0.71; in language classes mostly teacher reports |
| Main criticism | Little new language for learners with little exposure | Too little guidance for novices | Product and content can crowd out language; causal evidence is thin |
Sources: Ellis (2009, 2017); Long (2016); Bryfonski & McKay (2019); Swan (2005); Barrows (1996); Savery (2006); Kirschner et al. (2006); Hmelo-Silver et al. (2007); Strobel & van Barneveld (2009); Beckett & Slater (2005); Stoller & Myers (2019); Kokotsaki et al. (2016); Chen & Yang (2019).

## Where they meet
kind: card
- **Problem-solving** is one of the common pedagogic task types in TBLT, so a PBL problem can be a large task.
- A PBL or PjBL unit can be built from a **sequence of tasks** that ends in an exit task — the solution or the product.
- In a language class, PBL and PjBL both need a planned **language focus**, or the content and the product can crowd out the language.
refs: ellis2017 | long2016 | beckett2005 | stoller2019

## Choosing between them
kind: card
- **Beginners, little class time:** short tasks, input-based first, with task-supported practice of new language.
- **A real question to answer, more time, content goals:** a problem (PBL), with scaffolds and a language focus.
- **A real audience to make something for:** a project (PjBL) built from tasks, with the language made explicit.
- **Either way:** decide what evidence would show it worked — a task outcome, a language measure, or both.
refs: ellis2017 | swan2005 | kirschner2006

## Task-based assessment
kind: card
Norris (2016) argues that tasks are a strong — though not the only — basis for language tests, because they show what learners can **do** with the language. That raises Aryadoust's question: what does a task score mean, and does it predict performance on other tasks? The same questions apply to the GPT speaking examiner, which scores learners' performance in a conversation.
refs: norris2016 | long2016 | argval | gptspeak

## Make your chatbot a task
kind: card
Check your Mizou bot against the four criteria. A quiz bot asks questions with right answers; a **task** bot holds information the learner needs and asks for a decision.
refs: ellis2009 | ellis2017

## Mizou template · a task, not a quiz
kind: prompt
Pre-task, task and language focus in one bot. Change the scenario to fit your learners.
```text
You are a staff member at a youth hostel in [city]. The learner is planning a two-night class trip for [number] students with a budget of [amount].
Pre-task: greet the learner, explain the goal in one sentence, and offer three useful phrases for asking about prices and options.
Task: you know three rooms and two activities with prices and rules (invent them; keep them consistent). Give information only when asked. The learner must choose one room and one activity within budget and give a reason.
Do not correct language during the task, unless the learner cannot be understood; then ask a clarification question.
When the learner has decided, confirm the booking (the outcome).
Language focus: then show two sentences the learner wrote and a better version of each, and one phrase to use next time.
Use English at [level]. Keep every reply under 60 words. Do not ask for personal information.
```
