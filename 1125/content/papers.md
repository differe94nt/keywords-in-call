## argval
title: A critical assessment of the argument-based validity framework
short: Aryadoust & Zakaria (2026)
cite: Book chapter in *Effective Assessment in Schools* (Routledge)
badge: Book chapter · 2026
themes: teach | test
home: 3 | 7
rq: Aim (from the abstract): to interrogate the argument-based validity framework that dominates validation theory in language assessment — its relevance for practice and its limitations.
method: A conceptual chapter. It traces how mainstream validity theory developed and surfaces its assumptions, then contrasts validity as understood in educational measurement (**assessment of learning**) with ideas of validity developed from classroom practice (**assessment for learning** and **assessment as learning**).
found:
- The dominant framework grew out of measurement for ranking and certifying; its assumptions do not automatically fit classroom assessment.
- How you define validity shapes what you count as good assessment practice.
- The authors invite readers to rethink validity for their own contexts and suggest ways this rethinking can improve practice.
design:
- Before building a quiz or rubric, decide its **purpose**: to rank (of learning), to guide teaching (for learning) or to help learners regulate themselves (as learning). Each purpose needs different evidence.
- For a classroom AI chatbot that gives feedback, the key validity question is whether learners can *use* the feedback, not only whether its scores are accurate.
basis: Publisher abstract only.
access: No free full text — use the NTU library or interlibrary loan.
refs: argval

## relmeta
title: A meta-analysis of the role of reliability in inter-construct correlations in second language research
short: Zhao & Aryadoust (2026)
cite: *Educational Assessment*, advance online publication
badge: Meta-analysis · 2026
themes: test | R
home: 3 | 6
rq: Aim (from the abstract): how does the reliability of a test condition the size of the correlations researchers observe between that test and other constructs?
method: Meta-analysis of **49 studies (1999–2025)**: 58 Cronbach's alpha estimates and 463 correlations between **reading comprehension tests** and 13 other language-related measures, analysed with robust variance estimation to handle dependent effect sizes.
found:
- Cronbach's alpha significantly predicted the size of inter-construct correlations (*t*(21.74) = 2.35, *p* = .03).
- Less reliable reading tests systematically produced **weaker (attenuated) correlations** with other measures.
- Measurement error does not just add noise; it limits what relationships researchers can observe.
design:
- A short or poorly written classroom test can make two skills look unrelated when they are related. Report reliability before interpreting any correlation.
- When you read a study claiming "vocabulary hardly predicts reading", check the reliability of its tests first.
basis: Publisher abstract only.
access: No free full text yet (published online October 2026).
refs: relmeta

## typology
title: The typology of second language listening constructs: A systematic review
short: Aryadoust & Luo (2023)
cite: *Language Testing*, 40(2)
badge: Systematic review · 2023
themes: test | teach | L
home: 4 | 3
rq: Aim (from the abstract): how have L2 listening constructs been conceptualized (defined) and operationalized (measured) in published research?
method: Systematic review of **157 peer-reviewed papers in 19 applied linguistics journals**, coded for publication details and reliability, and for the listening subskills, cognitive processes, attributes and listening functions each study measured or investigated.
found:
- Only **39 papers (24.84%)** gave a theoretical definition of listening, and 38 of those were general or narrow.
- Listening functions such as **discriminative, empathetic and analytical listening** were largely ignored.
- The studies operationalized **24 subskills, 27 cognitive processes and 54 listening attributes** (105 features).
- The authors built a **multilayered framework**: subskills and processes in five groups each, attributes in three groups.
design:
- Write down what *kind* of listening your task targets before you write the questions — gist, detail, inference, or a listening function such as empathetic listening.
- Use the framework as a checklist so a listening test does not measure only one narrow slice of the skill.
basis: Publisher abstract only.
access: The NIE repository record says an open version is at the publisher; we could not confirm it. Try the DOI link on campus.
refs: typology

## subskills
title: A review of comprehension subskills: A scientometrics perspective
short: Aryadoust (2020)
cite: *System*, 88, Article 102180
badge: Scientometric review · 2020
themes: test | teach | L | R
home: 4 | 6 | 8
rq: Aim (from the paper): no universally accepted taxonomy of listening and reading **comprehension subskills** exists, so the study (i) identifies influential publications on comprehension subskills objectively, through keyword searches and citation analysis, and (ii) reviews their content to list the subskills that have been empirically investigated.
method: **Document co-citation analysis** of 192 publications (Scopus and Web of Science, merged), with timeline visualization, burst detection and automatic cluster labelling; then a close reading of the influential publications in each cluster.
found:
- **18 substantial research clusters** containing **73 empirically investigated subskills** — 55 in L1 comprehension, only 18 in L2.
- 15 clusters (83.33%) focused on **lower-order L1 reading** abilities such as orthographic processing and speeded word reading.
- The subskills were drawn as a **codex**, the first integrative framework of empirically investigated comprehension subskills.
- Experimental research on **L2** comprehension subskills is needed.
design:
- Teachers of young L2 learners can include activities that teach and check lower-level abilities — phonological awareness, rapid naming, lexical access — to find learners' gaps.
- Use the codex and its reference clusters to choose which subskill a reading or listening item should target.
basis: Author's accepted manuscript (full text).
download: https://repository.nie.edu.sg/server/api/core/bitstreams/f53be6b0-d6d0-4fcc-bbe5-51bae4f0a102/content
access: Free · accepted manuscript, NIE repository
refs: subskills

## lcbook
title: An evaluation framework for second language listening coursebooks: Principles and practice
short: Zhai et al. (2026)
cite: *TEFLIN Journal*, 37(1)
badge: Materials framework · 2026
themes: mat | teach | L | AI
home: 4
rq: "(1) What good qualities should listening texts and tasks exhibit? (2) What cognitive processes should listening tasks engage learners in? (3) What level of difficulty should listening texts present to support learning?"
method: A conceptual framework grounded in listening comprehension, listening assessment and SLA theory. **Seven principles**, each with a guiding question and indicators, illustrated on Units 1–4 of *Listen This Way* (Level 4), a coursebook for second-year English majors in mainland China; text difficulty checked with Text Inspector.
found:
- The seven principles — texts: (1) authentic spoken features, (2) diverse accents, (3) multimodal, (4) difficulty matched to learners; tasks: (5) varied and real-world, (6) bottom-up **and** top-down processing, (7) metacognitive awareness.
- The coursebook met principles 1, 4 and 6, but used only British and American accents, offered few visuals, had no integrated listen-to-speak or listen-to-write tasks, and did little for metacognitive awareness.
design:
- Use the seven principles to evaluate — and then adapt — any listening material, including the materials you designed this semester.
- Fill gaps with unscripted podcasts, other accents of English, video, integrated tasks, listening diaries and self-assessment checklists.
- Generative AI can draft texts and tasks, synthesize several accents and make video — always with human checking.
- The authors note that the framework is conceptual and not yet empirically validated.
basis: Full text.
download: https://journal.teflin.org/index.php/journal/article/download/3375/456
access: Free · published version, CC BY-SA
refs: lcbook

## llmlisten
title: Investigating the affordances of OpenAI's large language model in developing listening assessments
short: Aryadoust, Zakaria & Jia (2024)
cite: *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence*, 6, Article 100204
badge: Empirical study · 2024
themes: test | mat | L | AI
home: 4 | 8 | 3
rq: Aim (from the abstract): can ChatGPT 4 write listening scripts and test items for test takers at different proficiency levels (academic, low, intermediate, advanced), to reduce the cost and complexity of listening test development?
method: Prompt engineering and step-by-step refinement of prompts to generate scripts and items on **24 topics** taken from academic listening tests. Scripts were analysed linguistically with **Coh-Metrix and Text Inspector**; items were analysed for topic variation and overlap. Goh & Aryadoust (2025) report the output as 96 scripts and 384 multiple-choice items.
found:
- ChatGPT 4 **reliably varied the scripts' language** by level, as the prompts required.
- The **items were weaker**: often long, with options that overlapped in meaning; quality also depended on the topic.
- Goh & Aryadoust (2025) add that many options could be eliminated through world knowledge alone.
design:
- AI is a good **script writer** for levelled listening texts; check them with a text profiler.
- Treat AI-written **items** as drafts: shorten them, remove overlapping options, and check that no item can be answered without listening.
- The paper also discusses the ethical questions of using GenAI in assessment.
basis: Abstract; item counts and the world-knowledge finding as reported in Goh & Aryadoust (2025).
download: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X24000055
access: Free · open access, CC BY-NC-ND (download on the publisher page)
refs: llmlisten

## goh2025
title: Developing and assessing second language listening and speaking: Does AI make it better?
short: Goh & Aryadoust (2025)
cite: *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics*, 45
badge: Review · 2025
themes: teach | test | L | S | AI
home: 4 | 5 | 8
rq: Aim: how can AI — especially generative AI — support the teaching, learning and assessment of L2 listening and speaking, and what are its limits? (A review with no numbered research questions.)
method: A critical narrative review organised around models of listening (perception, parsing, utilization) and speaking (conceptualization, formulation, articulation), covering spoken dialogue systems, personal assistants, chatbots, custom GPTs and AI-generated tests.
found:
- Listening has received less GenAI attention than other skills; studies of voice assistants mostly found no significant listening gains.
- Chatbots and spoken dialogue systems consistently **raise willingness to communicate and lower anxiety**, but evidence of actual language development is thin.
- AI-generated listening tests have **authenticity and cognitive-validity problems**.
- Custom GPTs can give corrective, recast and self-regulation feedback, or act as a speaking examiner.
- Risks: privacy of voice and student data, especially for young learners, and hallucination.
design:
- Use GenAI for **assessment for and as learning** — personalised materials, immediate feedback, support for self-regulation — not only for ranking.
- Let learners repeat speaking tasks with an AI partner, but keep face-to-face interaction: AI talk does not build the full repertoire of interaction skills.
- Use AI for pre-task planning (ideas, vocabulary), not for scripts; teach AI literacy; keep the teacher as **the human in the loop**, evaluating AI tools as carefully as textbooks.
basis: Full text.
download: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8F12356CE315EDC807C1C82F3DBCB7A9/S0267190525100111a.pdf/div-class-title-developing-and-assessing-second-language-listening-and-speaking-does-ai-make-it-better-div.pdf
access: Free · published version, CC BY-NC-ND

## gptspeak
title: Automatic conversational assessment using a GPT-based model for EAP speaking
short: Aryadoust et al. (2026)
cite: *English for Specific Purposes*, 84
badge: Empirical study · 2026
themes: test | S | AI
home: 5 | 3 | 8
rq: Aim (from the abstract): how can a conversational agent, customized through advanced prompting in OpenAI's **GPT Builder**, be used to assess speaking in English for academic purposes? The system was built around four dimensions: measurement definition, system design, performance evaluation and learner experience.
method: First-year English majors from two large universities took a GPT-run speaking assessment in which they could **choose topics, control their response time and ask for clarification**. GPT and human ratings were compared with **many-facet Rasch measurement** and linear mixed-effects models, across eight scoring categories.
found:
- GPT was a **mid-range rater in severity**; mean differences from human raters were not significant.
- **3 of the 8 scoring categories** differed significantly between GPT and human ratings.
- GPT scoring was acceptably consistent and reliable, with typical rater habits such as avoiding extreme scores.
- Students who asked for clarification tended to score lower; students who spoke longer tended to score higher.
- Students' views of GPT did not affect their scores.
design:
- A custom GPT examiner is a realistic option for **formative** speaking assessment, especially where learners choose topics and pace.
- Check its ratings against human raters category by category: agreement overall can hide disagreement on specific criteria.
- Decide in advance how clarification requests should count — the study found they went with lower scores.
basis: Abstract only (the sample size and category names were not available to us).
download: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0889490626000451
access: Free · open access, CC BY-NC (download on the publisher page); a preprint is also on SSRN
refs: gptspeak

## ylgenai
title: Formative assessment of speaking for young learners using customized generative AI
short: Aryadoust & Zakaria (2026)
cite: Book chapter in *Second Language Oracy Development for Young Learners* (Springer)
badge: Book chapter · 2026
themes: teach | test | S | AI
home: 5 | 8
rq: Aim (from the chapter page): how can a customized generative AI (ChatGPT) be designed to provide formative speaking assessment for young learners?
method: A conceptual and design chapter grounded in communicative competence theory and models of formative feedback. It walks through building a customized GPT — **task specifications, tone, feedback design and iterative testing** — with sample interactions and classroom uses.
found:
- Direct and semi-direct speaking tests struggle with feasibility, cost and construct underrepresentation; speaking needs an interlocutor.
- A customized GPT can give feedback at the **task, process, self-regulation and self** levels; learners can review their speech through transcripts.
- Limits: data privacy, **age restrictions**, no multimodal input, and the transparency and reliability of automated scoring.
- It "cannot replace human interlocutors" but can extend speaking practice at low cost.
design:
- Design the bot's feedback by level: what to say about the task, about the process, and about the learner's own planning and monitoring.
- Check the tool's age rules before children use it; young learners usually need a teacher-run setup.
basis: Chapter abstract page only.
access: No free full text — use the NTU library.
refs: ylgenai

## visflu
title: Investigating the role of AI-generated visual input in predicting perceived fluency
short: Jia & Aryadoust (2026, preprint)
cite: SSRN preprint — not yet peer reviewed
badge: Preprint · 2026
themes: test | S | AI
home: 5 | 3
rq: Aim (from the abstract): does visual input change how raters perceive speech fluency — their ratings and their response times — and how do response times relate to ratings?
method: Within-subjects experiment: **82 participants rated 30 AI-generated speech stimuli**, each presented in audio-only and audiovisual conditions in random order; linear mixed-effects models plus correlations.
found:
- AI-generated visual input **sped up raters' responses but did not change fluency ratings**.
- The authors suggest matching visuals give "redundant" information already in the audio.
- Response time correlated with ratings in simple correlations, but not once rater and stimulus variation were controlled.
design:
- Adding a talking-head video to a speaking task may change *how* raters work without changing scores — useful evidence when choosing between audio and video formats.
- AI-generated speakers can be research and practice material, with limits the authors discuss.
- Simple correlations can mislead when each rater scores many samples.
basis: Abstract only. This is a preprint; check whether a peer-reviewed version has appeared before citing it.
download: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6235281
access: Free · preprint on SSRN
refs: visflu

## ann
title: Does EFL readers' lexical and grammatical knowledge predict their reading ability? Insights from a perceptron artificial neural network study
short: Aryadoust & Baghaei (2016)
cite: *Educational Assessment*, 21(2)
badge: Empirical study · 2016
themes: test | R | AI
home: 6 | 3 | 8
rq: Aim (from the abstract): how well do EFL learners' vocabulary and grammar knowledge distinguish low- from high-ability readers?
method: **825 test takers** took an L2 reading test plus validated grammar and vocabulary tests; abilities were estimated with the **Rasch model**; a multilayer **perceptron artificial neural network** classified readers as low or high ability from their grammar and vocabulary measures.
found:
- The network correctly classified about **78% of readers** from vocabulary and grammar alone.
- **Vocabulary** was more strongly associated with reading comprehension than grammar.
- The results fit cognitive theories that treat lexical and grammatical knowledge as a major difference between poor and competent readers.
design:
- Vocabulary and grammar tests can serve as a quick screening of reading readiness — but about one reader in five would be misclassified, so do not use them alone.
- In reading materials, vocabulary support is the first lever to pull.
- An early example of machine learning in language assessment, years before generative AI.
basis: Publisher abstract only.
access: No free full text — use the NTU library.
refs: ann

## awesr
title: A systematic review of automated writing evaluation systems
short: Shi & Aryadoust (2023)
cite: *Education and Information Technologies*, 28(1)
badge: Systematic review · 2023
themes: test | W | AI
home: 7 | 3
rq: "1. What are the study contexts and the trends of AWE scoring … 2. Which validity inferences have been investigated in the identified AWE studies? 3. What methodologies were adopted in the AWE studies to investigate each validity inference? 4. What areas of investigation have been studied to provide evidence of backing or rebuttals for each inference?"
method: Systematic review of **105 published papers on automated writing evaluation (AWE) scoring** from Scopus, coded within an **argument-based validation framework** (domain description, evaluation, generalization, explanation, extrapolation, utilization).
found:
- Research is growing and spans many settings, languages and educational levels.
- Coverage of the validity inferences is uneven: **evaluation** (scoring accuracy) is studied most; **domain description** is neglected.
- Most studies were quantitative and positive, but **counter-evidence appeared for every inference**.
design:
- Replacing human raters with AWE is not justified; combine AWE with human scoring for high-stakes use, or use it as an independent rater for immediate formative scores.
- Before adopting a tool, ask what writing **domain** it was built for and whether that matches your learners' tasks.
basis: Author's accepted manuscript (full text).
download: https://repository.nie.edu.sg/server/api/core/bitstreams/223f6952-ce18-4df4-8f63-b51cd2f44141/content
access: Free · accepted manuscript, NIE repository
refs: awesr

## awfsr
title: A systematic review of AI-based automated written feedback research
short: Shi & Aryadoust (2024)
cite: *ReCALL*, 36(2)
badge: Systematic review · 2024
themes: teach | test | W | AI
home: 7 | 8 | 3
rq: "1. What are the research contexts in AWF studies … 2. What types of AWF provided by AWF systems were investigated, and in what ways was AWF utilized in these studies? 3. How can the published AWF research be characterized in terms of the research design … 4. What are the foci of investigation in the studies, and what results are found?"
method: Systematic review (PRISMA) of **83 SSCI articles (85 studies, 1993–2022)** from Scopus, coded on 4 categories and 17 variables; intra-coder agreement above κ = .90; findings interpreted through the argument-based validity framework.
found:
- 82.4% of studies were at university level; English was the target language in 82 of 85.
- **31 systems**, led by Pigai, Criterion and Grammarly; 57.6% of studies looked at **form-focused** feedback, only 15 at meaning.
- 14 ways of using AWF — most often on its own, sometimes compared with teacher or peer feedback.
- Effects on writing were mostly positive (30 of 38 studies), but **validation of AWF accuracy had the least research and the weakest results**.
design:
- Form-focused AWF cannot cover the whole writing construct; add teacher or peer feedback on content, organisation and coherence.
- AWF "may hardly be the equivalent of or replacement for" human feedback — decide whether your tool supplements or replaces.
- Check a tool's stated users, levels and genres before using it; the authors point to generative AI for higher-order feedback.
basis: Full text.
download: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/28A670C4C7F2F1F30C7EA36EC489F867/S0958344023000265a.pdf/div-class-title-a-systematic-review-of-ai-based-automated-written-feedback-research-div.pdf
access: Free · published version, CC BY

## callsci
title: A scientometric review of research trends in computer-assisted language learning (1977–2020)
short: Lim & Aryadoust (2022)
cite: *Computer Assisted Language Learning*, 35(9)
badge: Scientometric review · 2022
themes: teach | mat
home: 8
rq: Aim (from the abstract): what have been the main research trends in CALL from 1977 to 2020, and how much support is there for the technologies studied?
method: **Document co-citation analysis** of **3,697 studies in 11 journals** (Scopus), with burst index and betweenness centrality to identify influential publications, then content analysis of those publications.
found:
- **Seven major research clusters**.
- Stronger support for **synchronous computer-mediated communication and negotiated interaction, multimedia, telecollaboration or e-mail exchange, and digital games**.
- Weaker support, and some major drawbacks, for **blogs, wikis and podcasts**.
design:
- When choosing a technology for a lesson, start from the clusters with stronger evidence, and ask what kind of interaction the tool creates.
- The study is also a ready-made map of research topics for your final project.
basis: Publisher abstract only.
access: No free full text — use the NTU library.
refs: callsci

## dst
title: A systematic review of digital storytelling in language learning in adolescents and adults
short: Lim, Zakaria & Aryadoust (2022)
cite: *Education and Information Technologies*, 27(5)
badge: Systematic review · 2022
themes: teach | mat | L | S | R | W
home: 8 | 7
rq: "1. What are the research topics and methods that have been adopted in previous DST research? 2. What are the theories or frameworks of DST that have been adopted in previous DST research? 3. How were the studies designed in terms of the reliability of the instruments used? 4. How do the outcomes of using DST vary across the four language skills and variables in the coding scheme?"
method: Systematic review of digital storytelling (DST) studies with adolescents and adults found in Scopus — 71 papers in the dataset, 58 after removing 13 repeated entries — coded for topics, methods, theories, reliability and outcomes in the four skills.
found:
- Combining several data-collection methods was the most common approach (23 of 58 studies, 39.7%); most studies neither tested nor mentioned a theory or framework.
- **Close to 90% of studies reported no reliability testing** of their instruments.
- Most reported positive outcomes, but "not all claims were supported with evidence"; listening and speaking studies were few.
design:
- Digital storytelling is promising for all four skills, but the evidence is weaker than its popularity suggests.
- If you use it, define the components (images, audio, script), the skill you are developing and how you will measure it — reliably.
basis: Author's accepted manuscript (full text).
download: https://repository.nie.edu.sg/server/api/core/bitstreams/50f00c34-3704-4309-bdf7-7c0e0b5a1224/content
access: Free · accepted manuscript, NIE repository
