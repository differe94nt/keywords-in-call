Every tool on this site raises the same handful of questions. Answer them in your proposal — reviewers and examiners will ask.

## The issues
kind: section

## Accuracy
kind: issue
why: Generated texts, answer keys and summaries can be fluent and wrong — including invented citations, as you saw in Week 2.
do: Check every fact and every answer key yourself. Cite only sources you have opened. Keep a record of what you corrected.

## Learner data and privacy
kind: issue
why: Free tiers may use what you submit to improve products, and Google's free AI Studio terms say human reviewers may read inputs and ask users not to submit personal information.
do: Never paste learners' names, writing, voice recordings or test results into a free AI tool. Anonymise first, or analyse by hand. For research, say in your consent form where data goes.

## Age and consent
kind: issue
why: Several tools are 18+ only, and some terms forbid using them in anything likely to reach children.
do: With under-18 learners, keep AI on the teacher's side or use a version that makes no AI calls. For research with minors, get school and parent or guardian consent.

## Accessibility
kind: issue
why: Interactive material can shut learners out — audio without captions, colour-only feedback, tiny buttons, timers.
do: Captions or transcripts for all audio and video; text alternatives for images; keyboard operation; no colour-only signals. For Deaf or hard-of-hearing learners, make every piece of information visual and ask them what they actually use — do not assume. The UDL guidelines are a good checklist.
link: [UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/)

## Copyright and licences
kind: issue
why: Uploading a textbook chapter, a paywalled article or a corpus with a no-redistribution licence to a public tool may break its terms. Generated images can resemble existing work.
do: Upload only material you have the right to use. Link to sources instead of copying them into shared materials. Credit images and say which ones were generated.

## Access and reliability
kind: issue
why: Accounts may be blocked on school networks, free limits run out, links break, Wi-Fi fails during the demonstration.
do: Test on the actual device and account you will use. Always bring a version that needs no internet.

## Over-reliance
kind: issue
why: If the tool does the thinking, learners — and you — practise less. A finished-looking product can hide that nobody learned anything.
do: Design tasks where learners must produce language themselves, and check what they can do without the tool at the end.

## Honesty about AI use
kind: issue
why: Your readers need to know which parts are yours. AI detectors are not a dependable check — one study found they frequently misclassified essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated — so transparency matters more than detection.
do: Include an AI-use statement in your proposal, presentation and portfolio: which tools, for what, and how you checked the output.
refs: liang2023

## A short AI-use statement
kind: prompt
Adapt this for your proposal or portfolio.
```text
AI use: I used [tool] on [date] to [purpose, e.g. draft the vocabulary worksheet / extract study details from my five sources]. I checked [what, e.g. every answer key and every citation against the original paper] and changed [what]. The design decisions, the analysis and the final text are my own.
```
