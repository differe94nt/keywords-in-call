## CALL — Computer-assisted language learning
match: computer-assisted language learning | computer assisted language learning | CALL
line: Any use of computers to learn or teach a second language.
definition: The term the field started with, and still its widest umbrella. Its own history tracks the field's: drill software, then communicative practice, then integration into ordinary teaching. Bax's question — whether technology has become invisible like the pen — is still the live one.
unit: A programme or a classroom
method: Position papers, surveys, classroom case studies
evidence: Causal claims, because "CALL versus no CALL" has no clean comparison condition
search: ("computer assisted language learning" OR CALL) AND (normalisation OR normalization OR "technology integration")
check: The acronym collides with the ordinary verb "call". Quote the full phrase, or pair the acronym with a second term.
contested:
- Has technology normalised, or has the label simply been replaced every ten years?
- Is CALL a field, a method, or just a setting where ordinary SLA questions get asked?
sources:
- Warschauer, M., & Healey, D. (1998). Computers and language learning: An overview. *Language Teaching*.
- Bax, S. (2003). CALL — past, present and future. *System*.
- Chambers, A., & Bax, S. (2006). Making CALL work: Towards normalisation. *System*.
- Chapelle, C. A. (2001). *Computer applications in second language acquisition*. Cambridge University Press.

## TELL — Technology-enhanced language learning
match: technology-enhanced language learning | technology enhanced language learning | TELL
line: CALL renamed, with a claim hidden in the label.
definition: Widens the frame from the computer to any digital technology. The word "enhanced" smuggles in an outcome: enhanced compared with what, measured how? Run the same query as CALL and as TELL, then compare the top twenty results — the overlap is itself a finding.
unit: The tool
method: Effectiveness reviews, mixed methods
evidence: The construct itself — reviews mix tool studies with pedagogy studies
search: ("technology enhanced language learning" OR TELL OR "digital language learning") AND (review OR "systematic review" OR meta-analysis)
check: TELL as an acronym retrieves the verb "tell" constantly. Use the full phrase first, then add the acronym.
contested:
- Does TELL retrieve a different literature from CALL, or the same papers with newer dates?
- "Enhancement" is an outcome claim baked into a keyword. What is the baseline?
sources:
- Golonka, E. M., Bowles, A. R., Frank, V. M., Richardson, D. L., & Freynik, S. (2014). Technologies for foreign language learning: A review of technology types and their effectiveness. *Computer Assisted Language Learning*.
- Macaro, E., Handley, Z., & Walter, C. (2012). A systematic review of CALL in English as a second language classrooms. *Language Teaching*.
- Stockwell, G. (2012). *Computer-assisted language learning: Diversity in research and practice*. Cambridge University Press.
- Godwin-Jones, R. Emerging Technologies column, *Language Learning & Technology* (ongoing).

## CMC — Computer-mediated communication
match: computer-mediated communication | computer mediated communication | CMC | telecollaboration | virtual exchange
line: Learning through other people, with a machine in between.
definition: Text chat, video calls, forums, class-to-class exchange across countries. Synchronous and asynchronous behave differently enough that collapsing them distorts any summary. Interactionist claims about noticing and negotiation were built on face-to-face data, and whether they transfer is an empirical question rather than an assumption.
unit: The interactional turn
method: Discourse analysis, quasi-experiments
evidence: Transfer beyond the task, and intercultural gains that are hard to measure
search: ("computer mediated communication" OR SCMC OR ACMC OR telecollaboration OR "virtual exchange") AND (interaction OR negotiation OR "second language")
check: Check whether a study's "chat" is text or video before grouping it with others.
contested:
- Does text chat buy learners planning time, or remove the pressure that drives fluency?
- Is video CMC weaker than face to face because of missing cues, or because of the task design?
sources:
- Warschauer, M. (1996). Comparing face-to-face and electronic discussion in the second language classroom. *CALICO Journal*.
- Blake, R. (2000). Computer mediated communication: A window on L2 Spanish interlanguage. *Language Learning & Technology*.
- Ziegler, N. (2016). Synchronous computer-mediated communication and interaction: A meta-analysis. *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*.
- O'Dowd, R. (2018). From telecollaboration to virtual exchange: State-of-the-art and the role of UNICollaboration. *Journal of Virtual Exchange*.

## MALL — Mobile-assisted language learning
match: mobile-assisted language learning | mobile assisted language learning | MALL | mobile learning
line: Learning on a device the learner already carries.
definition: Handheld, personal, always present — which changes not only where learning happens but who decides when. Most of the evidence is short, small and vocabulary-focused, so the honest question is whether mobility is doing the work or whether it is spaced repetition delivered conveniently.
unit: The learner's session
method: Short quasi-experiments, app trials
evidence: Duration, control groups, and what mobility adds beyond convenience
search: ("mobile assisted language learning" OR MALL OR "mobile learning") AND (vocabulary OR speaking OR listening) AND (experiment* OR quasi-experimental)
check: Date-limit hard. Pre-2012 MALL usually means SMS on feature phones — a different object of study.
contested:
- Is the interesting variable mobility, or spaced repetition?
- App studies measure engagement, then report engagement as success. Is that a finding?
sources:
- Kukulska-Hulme, A., & Shield, L. (2008). An overview of mobile assisted language learning: From content delivery to supported collaboration and interaction. *ReCALL*.
- Burston, J. (2015). Twenty years of MALL project implementation: A meta-analysis of learning outcomes. *ReCALL*.
- Sung, Y.-T., Chang, K.-E., & Yang, J.-M. (2015). How effective are mobile devices for language learning? A meta-analysis. *Educational Research Review*.
- Stockwell, G. (2010). Using mobile phones for vocabulary activities: Examining the effect of the platform. *Language Learning & Technology*.

## AWE — Automated writing evaluation
match: automated writing evaluation | AWE | automated feedback | essay scoring
line: Writing judged without a human reader.
definition: Essay scoring engines, grammar checkers, and the feedback layer now attached to every writing tool. Agreement with human raters is often high, which tells you the machine predicts the rating — not that the rating measures writing. Keep two questions apart: does the feedback produce revision, and does revision produce learning?
unit: The draft
method: Pre-post designs, corpora of revisions
evidence: Transfer to a new, unscaffolded task
search: ("automated writing evaluation" OR "automated essay scoring" OR "automated feedback") AND (L2 OR ESL OR EFL) AND (revision OR accuracy OR uptake)
check: Scoring produces a number, feedback produces a comment. Papers that conflate them will distort your summary.
contested:
- High agreement with human raters, and still possibly the wrong construct.
- Do learners over-trust the machine on content, or on grammar? Studies disagree.
sources:
- Warschauer, M., & Ware, P. (2006). Automated writing evaluation: Defining the classroom research agenda. *Language Teaching Research*.
- Stevenson, M., & Phakiti, A. (2014). The effects of computer-generated feedback on the quality of writing. *Assessing Writing*.
- Li, J., Link, S., & Hegelheimer, V. (2015). Rethinking the role of automated writing evaluation (AWE) feedback in ESL writing instruction. *Journal of Second Language Writing*.
- Ranalli, J., Link, S., & Chukharev-Hudilainen, E. (2017). Automated writing evaluation for formative assessment of second language writing. *Computer Assisted Language Learning*.

## DGBLL — Digital game-based language learning
match: game-based language learning | game based language learning | DGBLL | gamification | digital game
line: Learning through play — and the thing that only looks like play.
definition: Commercial games repurposed for language, games built for teaching, and game mechanics bolted onto ordinary tasks. That last one is gamification, and points are not play. Vocabulary gains are reasonably documented; interaction, pragmatics and anything past one semester are not.
unit: The play session
method: Case studies, mixed methods, small trials
evidence: Isolating which mechanic does the work, and whether motivation survives novelty
search: ("game based language learning" OR DGBLL OR gamification OR MMORPG) AND ("second language" OR L2) AND (vocabulary OR motivation OR interaction)
check: Ask of every study whether the game was designed for learning or borrowed. It changes what the finding is about.
contested:
- Gamification and game-based learning are routinely merged in reviews. Should they be?
- Are motivation findings novelty effects with a short half-life?
sources:
- Cornillie, F., Thorne, S. L., & Desmet, P. (2012). Digital games for language learning: From hype to insight? *ReCALL*.
- Peterson, M. (2012). Learner interaction in a massively multiplayer online role playing game (MMORPG): A sociocultural discourse analysis. *ReCALL*.
- Reinders, H., & Wattana, S. (2014). Can I say something? The effects of digital game play on willingness to communicate. *Language Learning & Technology*.
- Sykes, J. M. (2018). Digital games and language teaching and learning. *Foreign Language Annals*.

## Multimodal — Multimodality and multimodal input
match: multimodality and multimodal input | multimodal | captions | subtitles | multimedia
line: Meaning arriving through more than one channel at once.
definition: Image, sound and text together, and the claim that combining them helps — up to the point where it costs more than it gives. Dual coding predicts the gain; cognitive load predicts the cost. Caption type is a treatment variable, not a detail: L1 subtitles, L2 captions and keyword captions are three literatures.
unit: The input episode
method: Lab experiments, eye-tracking, classroom trials
evidence: Listening development as something separate from comprehension on the day
search: (caption* OR subtitle* OR multimodal) AND ("second language" OR L2 OR EFL) AND (listening OR "vocabulary acquisition") AND (eye-track* OR comprehension OR retention)
check: "With subtitles" is not a specification. Record which language, which words, and on screen for how long.
contested:
- Captions aid comprehension. Do they aid listening development?
- Learners prefer multimodal input and sometimes remember less of it. Which do we design for?
sources:
- Mayer, R. E. (2009). *Multimedia learning* (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.
- Montero Perez, M., Van Den Noortgate, W., & Desmet, P. (2013). Captioned video for L2 listening and vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis. *System*.
- Winke, P., Gass, S., & Sydorenko, T. (2010). The effects of captioning videos used for foreign language listening activities. *Language Learning & Technology*.
- Vanderplank, R. (2016). *Captioned media in foreign language learning and teaching*. Palgrave Macmillan.

## Autonomy — Learner autonomy and self-regulated learning with technology
match: learner autonomy | self-regulated learning | self regulated learning | autonomy
line: What learners do when nobody is watching.
definition: Choosing resources, setting goals, monitoring progress — and whether technology supports that capacity or quietly assumes it. Autonomy comes from a political tradition and names a capacity; self-regulation comes from educational psychology and names a process. Databases treat them as synonyms; they are not.
unit: The learner over time
method: Questionnaires, diaries, interviews, log data
evidence: Behavioural evidence, as opposed to self-reported strategy use
search: (autonomy OR "self-regulated learning" OR "self-directed learning") AND ("language learning") AND (technology OR online OR "out of class")
check: If all five of your studies are questionnaire studies, your summary is about beliefs, not behaviour. Say so.
contested:
- Access to resources is not the ability to use them. Does technology widen the gap it promises to close?
- Which regulation actually fails in digital study — planning, or time and effort?
sources:
- Benson, P. (2011). *Teaching and researching autonomy* (2nd ed.). Routledge.
- Zimmerman, B. J. (2000). Attaining self-regulation: A social cognitive perspective. In *Handbook of self-regulation*.
- Reinders, H., & White, C. (2016). 20 years of autonomy and technology: How far have we come and where to next? *Language Learning & Technology*.
- Lai, C. (2017). *Autonomous language learning with technology: Beyond the classroom*. Bloomsbury.

## TPACK — Teacher knowledge and digital competence
match: TPACK | technological pedagogical content knowledge | teacher digital competence | digital competence
line: What a teacher has to know to teach a language with technology.
definition: The frameworks that try to specify that knowledge, measure it, and build it through professional development. The seven components are hard to separate with real instruments, and most of the literature is generic education rather than language teaching — which is exactly where a thesis fits.
unit: The teacher
method: Survey instruments, PD case studies
evidence: Observed practice, and anything language-specific
search: (TPACK OR "technological pedagogical content knowledge" OR "digital competence") AND ("language teacher*" OR "EFL teacher*") AND (development OR training)
check: Check whether the instrument was validated with language teachers or borrowed from science education.
contested:
- Self-reported competence rises after training while observed practice does not. Which do we believe?
- Can the seven components be measured separately at all?
sources:
- Mishra, P., & Koehler, M. J. (2006). Technological pedagogical content knowledge: A framework for teacher knowledge. *Teachers College Record*.
- Hubbard, P. (2008). CALL and the future of language teacher education. *CALICO Journal*.
- Redecker, C. (2017). *European framework for the digital competence of educators: DigCompEdu*. Publications Office of the European Union.
- Tseng, J.-J., Chai, C. S., Tan, L., & Park, M. (2022). A critical review of research on technological pedagogical and content knowledge in language teaching. *Computer Assisted Language Learning*.

## GenAI — Generative AI and chatbots in language learning
match: generative AI | chatbot | ChatGPT | large language model | conversational agent
line: The tutor, the writing partner, and the thing summarising this literature.
definition: Dialogue systems and large language models as interlocutor, tutor and co-writer. The word "chatbot" spans two different technologies: rule-based systems before 2022 and language models after, so a set of five papers can contain both without saying so. Evidence moves faster than peer review, which is why preprints dominate.
unit: The dialogue or the text
method: Pilot studies, small trials, perception surveys, preprints
evidence: Replication, longer designs, and anything past self-reported perception
search: (chatbot OR "conversational agent" OR "large language model" OR ChatGPT OR "generative AI") AND ("language learning" OR "second language") AND (speaking OR writing OR feedback)
check: Date-limit hard, and check whether "AI" in a title means a transformer or a decision tree from 2015.
contested:
- Output fluency is not pedagogical quality. A model that never corrects you is pleasant and a poor tutor.
- How much pre-2022 dialogue-based CALL still applies to language models?
sources:
- Bibauw, S., François, T., & Desmet, P. (2019). Discussing with a computer: A systematic review of dialogue-based CALL. *Computer Assisted Language Learning*.
- Fryer, L. K., Ainley, M., Thompson, A., Gibson, A., & Sherlock, Z. (2017). Stimulating and sustaining interest in a language course: An experimental comparison of chatbot and human task partners. *Computers in Human Behavior*.
- Kohnke, L., Moorhouse, B. L., & Zou, D. (2023). ChatGPT for language teaching and learning. *RELC Journal*.
- Godwin-Jones, R. (2023). Emerging spaces for language learning: AI bots, ambient intelligence, and the metaverse. *Journal of China Computer-Assisted Language Learning*.
