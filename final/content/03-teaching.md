# Teaching demo track

You design a lesson for a specific group of learners, justify each design choice with the literature, and teach a **20-minute demonstration** to the class. Your classmates play the learners. The question you answer is not "is this activity fun?" but "why is this the right activity for *these* learners?"

## Know your learners
kind: section
Use the four lenses from Week 4. Write what you **know** (evidence), what you **think** (interpretation), and what you **still need to ask**.

## Readiness
kind: card
ask: What can the learners already do? Which words, structures and skills can you build on?
example: Grade 5 learners can name classroom objects but not yet describe where things are.

## Goals and context
kind: card
ask: What do they need English for, and in what setting will they use it?
example: A semester-abroad goal makes seminar participation a real target, not just "speaking practice".

## Access and participation
kind: card
ask: What helps or blocks participation — devices, Wi-Fi, time, vision, hearing, anxiety?
example: Learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing need every key piece of audio available as text or sign, and a visual way to check understanding. Ask what they use; do not assume.

## Choice and support
kind: card
ask: What choices and supports will you offer, and when will support be reduced?
example: Offer a word bank in round one, then remove it for the final task.

## A 20-minute plan
kind: section
Twenty minutes is short. Teach **one** learning goal well. A useful shape is below — change the timings to suit your goal, but keep a model, practice, and an independent check.

| Time | Stage | Learners do | Your material or tool | Why (theory) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Hook and goal | See the goal in learner-friendly words | Image, short clip, question | Activating prior knowledge |
| 3–8 | Model or input | Watch, listen or read a clear example | Multimodal input, captions | Multimedia learning; comprehensible input |
| 8–14 | Guided practice | Try with support: frames, word bank, partner | Interactive activity, game, worksheet | Scaffolding |
| 14–18 | Independent task | Use the language with less support, new content | Information-gap task, short production | Output and noticing |
| 18–20 | Check and close | Show what they can do; reflect | Exit question, quick quiz | Evidence of learning |

## Where AI fits — and where it doesn't
kind: section
Decide the learning action first, then ask whether AI makes it better. Name *who* uses the tool, because age rules differ: several tools are 18+ only, so with school-age learners AI usually belongs on the teacher's side.

## Teacher-side AI
kind: card
what: You use AI to *prepare*: levelled texts, image sets, worksheets, quiz items, an interactive activity you have tested.
check: Every item is checked by you before learners see it — facts, language level, answer keys, cultural fit.
example: A teacher-built picture-vocabulary game exported as a file that runs without calling an AI service.

## Learner-side AI
kind: card
what: Learners interact with AI during the lesson — a tutor, a feedback tool, a conversation partner.
check: Is the tool allowed for this age group and school? What data does it collect? What do learners do if its answer is wrong?
example: University learners revise a paragraph with AI feedback, then explain which suggestions they rejected and why.

## Showcase AI
kind: card
what: AI helps you *present* the material — an interactive demo, a slide deck, a short video.
check: The showcase should make the learning design clearer, not hide it behind effects.
example: An interactive HTML page that shows each stage of the lesson with the actual materials embedded.

## Theory you can build on
kind: section
You asked for more on **multimodality**. The word is used in three different traditions, and each one predicts something different for your design. Name the one you are using.

## Multimedia learning and dual coding
kind: theory
what: People process words and pictures through partly separate channels. Well-chosen pictures plus words can build two connected memory traces instead of one.
design: Pair each target word with an image that shows its meaning; put the label next to the image, not on a different screen; cut decorative pictures.
refs: mayer2009 | clark_paivio1991

## Cognitive load
kind: theory
what: Working memory is limited. Extra modes can help, but adding the same information twice (for example, on-screen text that repeats the narration word for word) or making learners hunt between sources can overload them.
design: Add a mode only when it carries information the learner needs. For beginners, shorter text and slower audio may help more than more media.
refs: sweller1988

## Multimodality as meaning-making
kind: theory
what: In social semiotics, meaning is made with many modes — image, layout, colour, gesture, sound, writing — and each mode does some things better than others. Learners are designers of meaning, not only receivers.
design: Ask learners to *make* a multimodal text (a captioned photo story, a labelled map) and explain why they chose each mode for each part of the message.
refs: kress2010

## Captions and subtitles
kind: theory
what: A meta-analysis found captioned video supports listening comprehension and vocabulary learning — and captions are also a matter of access for learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing.
design: Choose the caption type on purpose (full captions, keyword captions, or none) and say why.
refs: montero2013

## Scaffolding
kind: theory
what: Support that lets a learner complete a task they could not yet do alone — and that is reduced as they succeed.
design: Plan when each support (frame, word bank, model) is removed, and what you will do if a learner still needs it.
refs: wood1976

## Output and noticing
kind: theory
what: Producing language can make learners notice what they cannot yet say, which pushes them to reconsider their language.
design: Build a moment where learners must produce language for someone who needs it, then revise after a clarification question.
refs: swain_lapkin1995

## Learner fit
kind: theory
what: A task is appropriate when its difficulty and assumptions suit the learners you have — one of six criteria for judging CALL tasks.
design: Show in your plan how each material matches the learner analysis.
refs: chapelle2001

## Demo-day checklist
kind: checklist
- Rehearsed in full, timed, and under 20 minutes
- Every link and file tested on the classroom computer or your own laptop
- A backup that works with no internet: PDF, printed handout, or downloaded file
- Text large enough to read from the back; video captioned
- Classmates know who they are playing (age, level, situation)
- One sentence ready on what you would change after teaching it

## Questions to test your design
kind: questions
- Which detail in your learner analysis justifies your learning goal?
- If you removed the AI tool, what would learners lose?
- What will learners *say or write* during the lesson — and how much?
- How will you know, by minute 20, that the goal was reached?
- Which of your five sources would disagree with one of your choices?
