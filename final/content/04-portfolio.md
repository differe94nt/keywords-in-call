# Personal portfolio

The portfolio is **individual**. It is your reflection on the whole semester: what you learned, what you tried, what happened when you tried it, and how feedback from classmates changed your thinking. It is not a summary of the course — it is evidence of *your* learning.

**Format:** PowerPoint or HTML. If you choose HTML, the AI materials studio has a step-by-step guide to directing a language model to build a presentation page for you — we will practise it in class.

## What the portfolio must include
kind: checklist
- **A starting point:** what you believed about digital materials and AI in week 1
- **Three artifacts** from the semester, each with a short note on what it shows about your learning (for example your AI-summary audit, your material comparison table, a learner profile and lesson plan, project materials)
- **What you tried:** at least two tools or methods you used for the first time, and what happened
- **Feedback you received** from classmates — quoted or paraphrased — and what you changed because of it
- **Your final project:** your role, one decision you are proud of, one you would change
- **An AI-use statement:** which tools you used for the portfolio itself, and how you checked their output
- **Looking ahead:** one way this course will change how you teach or study

## Guided questions
kind: section
You do not need to answer every question. Choose the ones that let you tell the most honest story of your semester. Each set has a copy button so you can paste it into your drafting document.

## Where I started
kind: questions
- Before this course, what did "digital teaching material" mean to you? What did you think made one good?
- What did you believe AI could and could not do for language learners?
- Which keyword did you choose in week 2, and why that one?
- What were you most unsure about at the beginning of the semester?

## What I learned
kind: questions
- Which concept changed how you look at a material or an app — learner fit, authenticity, normalisation, scaffolding, multimodality? Explain the change with an example.
- When you compared your own summary with an AI summary in week 2, what did you notice that you had not expected?
- Which reading or source do you now cite most naturally, and what does it let you argue?
- What is one claim you used to accept that you now ask for evidence before believing?
- How would you explain the difference between "learners liked it" and "learners learned from it" to a colleague?

## What I tried
kind: questions
- Which tools did you use for the first time this semester? For each, name one thing it did well and one thing that went wrong.
- When did an AI tool give you something wrong or misleading? How did you find out, and what did you do?
- Which activity or material did you design, and what happened when classmates used it?
- What did you try that did not work — and what did you learn from it?
- If you used Gemini Notebook for your literature review, how did it change the way you read sources?

## Evidence of my learning
kind: questions
- Which three artifacts best show your growth? What does each one show that the others do not?
- Put an early piece of work next to a later one. What exactly is different?
- Where in your work can someone see you using a course concept, not just naming it?

## Feedback I received
kind: questions
- What is the most useful piece of feedback a classmate gave you this semester? Quote it if you can.
- Which feedback did you disagree with at first? Did you change your mind?
- What did you change in your project or materials because of feedback? Show the before and after.
- What feedback did *you* give that you think helped someone else?

## Looking ahead
kind: questions
- What kind of teacher, designer or researcher do you want to become, and how did this course move you toward it?
- Which learner group do you most want to design for next — and what would you need to learn first?
- What is one rule you will follow when you use AI in your own teaching?
- If you continued your final project for another semester, what would you do next?

## Keep a progress log
kind: card
what: Three lines after each class or work session, kept in one document all semester. It makes the portfolio easy to write, because the evidence is already collected — and it helps you see your own progress week by week.
example: **10/14** — Tried: elevator pitch for a multimodal vocabulary demo. Feedback: "Who are the learners exactly?" Next: write a one-paragraph learner profile before 10/28.
- **Date and what I tried**
- **What happened or what I noticed**
- **What I will do next**

## What a strong portfolio shows
kind: card
- **Depth:** explains *why* something changed, not only *that* it changed
- **Evidence:** every claim about your learning points to an artifact, a quotation or a before–after comparison
- **Feedback used:** shows a concrete change you made because of someone else's comment
- **Course concepts:** uses ideas from the readings to explain your own experience
- **Clear presentation:** readable, organised, accessible — captions for video, alt text for images, large enough text

## Privacy and honesty
kind: card
- Ask before naming a classmate or quoting their feedback with their name; otherwise quote it anonymously.
- Remove learner names and any identifying details from screenshots and data.
- Say which parts an AI tool drafted. A reflection is only useful if it is yours.
